When you buy a handmade piece, you are buying time: the days a maker spent on it. A price and a photograph can't show that time. Film can.

## What we film

Wherever we can, we film a piece from its first step to its last. For a cast statue, that means the wax model, the clay mould, the pour, the long days of chasing, and the gilding. For a thangka, it is the bare canvas, the grid, the first lines, layer after layer of colour, and finally the eyes.

We don't film every hour. We visit at each stage, and we record the days so that the piece's page can show its making to scale. On a 38-day statue, you can see that half the time went into the wax, and how little of it was the casting most people imagine.

## Three cuts of every film

Every film is cut three ways:

- **A 20-second cut** at the top of the piece's page, and for sharing.
- **About two minutes** in the making section, with chapters you can jump between.
- **The full, uncut record** on the maker's page, for anyone who wants to sit with the work.

Every film has captions in English and Nepali.

## When a piece was finished before we arrived

Some pieces were finished before we met them. For those, we film the same maker at the same work on another piece. The page says so plainly: "This film shows the same maker at the same work". We never present one piece's making as another's.

## Why it matters

A buyer who has watched a maker spend eleven days on a face looks at that face differently. Owners can write to the maker, and we hope some will write about exactly that moment. [Read how notes reach the workshop](/help/faq).
