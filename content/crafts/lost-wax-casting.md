## What it is

Lost-wax casting, also called *cire perdue*, makes a metal object from a wax original. The wax is covered in clay, melted out, and replaced by molten metal. It is used for sculpture, for ritual objects such as bells and lamps, and for ornaments and fittings. The wax is "lost" in the process, and the mould is broken to release the metal, so each mould makes one casting.

## Where and since when

Metal casting has continued without a break in the Newar heartland of the Kathmandu Valley. The earliest dated Nepalese metal sculpture known, a standing Buddha, is dated 591 CE, and Ian Alsop, who has studied the craft for decades, writes that the earliest metalwork is likely to be several centuries older.

In Patan, the craft has traditionally been practised by Newar families of the Buddhist bahals, notably the Śākyas, and casting and embossing workshops there still work for Hindu and Buddhist patrons. Patan sculptors usually model the wax themselves and cast their own work.

## How it is made

**The wax model.** The wax is a mix of beeswax, vegetable oil and tree resin, melted and rolled into sheets. Parts of the object are shaped from these sheets and joined into one wax original.

**The clay mould.** Several layers of fine and coarse clay are built up over the wax. The first layer is mixed with cow dung, which the Rubin Museum calls the most important layer for a good casting, and the coarse layers contain rice husks. Alsop notes that these organic materials make the mould porous enough to let gas escape. Each layer must dry before the next is added, which makes this the longest stage. An opening is left at the base for the wax to drain out and the metal to go in.

**Wax out and baking.** The wax is melted and drained away, and the empty clay mould is baked until it is hard.

**Pouring.** Hot metal is poured into the mould. Nepalese icons are cast almost exclusively in a nearly pure copper. When the metal has cooled, the mould is broken to reveal the casting.

**Finishing.** The rough casting is repaired where the metal has flowed badly, chiselled to bring out detail, and buffed. These steps are often done by separate specialists. Many pieces are then gilded, and some are set with semiprecious stones.

## Related technique: repoussé

Repoussé, or embossing, shapes sheet metal by hammering it over a form, heating it to keep it workable, and hammering again. Large works are made in parts and joined. Because casting uses more metal, large images and architectural decoration are usually embossed. Fine detail is then added with chisels.

## Gilding

Metal work in Nepal is usually gilded with the technique known as mercury gilding, also called fire-gilding. A paste of gold and mercury is applied, then heated until the mercury burns off, leaving gold on the surface, which is burnished. Mercury fumes are dangerous, and Alsop reports that mercury illness is common among gilders. Gold can also be applied by electroplating. Sīpa's page for each piece states which method was used.

## What to look for

- **Crisp detail** after chiselling, with no soft or rounded lines.
- **A clean surface:** no pits or patched areas left from the casting.
- **Even gilding,** burnished where it should catch the light.
- **Tidy joins** where parts were cast or embossed separately.

## Caring for it

Metal needs very little. Dust it with a soft, dry brush and do not polish it. [Read the metal care guide](/care/metal).
