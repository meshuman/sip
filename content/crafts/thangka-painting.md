## What it is

A thangka is a hanging scroll painted on prepared cloth or silk. It usually shows a deity, a scene or a mandala, and is used for teaching and for meditation. Because it can be rolled up, it is easy to carry and to store. In Nepal the Newar people have a related tradition, the paubha, which serves both Hindu and Buddhist communities. Thangka is the Tibetan Buddhist form.

## Where and since when

The origin of both paubha and thangka may be traced to Newar artists who worked in Tibet. The earliest known paubha, a painting of the Buddha Amitabha, is dated to the 11th century. Paubha painters traditionally belong to the Chitrakar community, known as Pun in Nepal Bhasa.

## How it is made

**Preparing the cloth.** The painter stitches the cloth to wooden rods and laces it onto a larger frame. A thin coat of weak glue goes on first, so that the paint does not crack, fade or soak into the fibres. A smooth base of size mixed with lime follows. When it is dry, the surface is rubbed several times with a smooth stone to polish it.

**Drawing to proportion.** Sacred figures follow fixed proportions set by religious texts, measured in relative units. The painter lays down gridlines, which let the figure be scaled while keeping its proportions, though experienced painters rarely need them. The figure is sketched in charcoal, with notes on colour, then drawn on the canvas and outlined in black ink.

**Colour.** Paint is made from mineral and plant pigments bound in animal glue. The painter works from the background to the centre. Water and clouds come first, one colour at a time, then the central figure, starting with the lotus throne, then the clothing, then the body, with light colours before dark.

**Shading and detail.** Shading is built up slowly, with gradual changes of tone, which the museum calls laborious work. Patterns on clothing and ornaments follow, with outlines in gold, red and black.

**Gold.** Gold is used for ornament and outlining. Some of it is polished so that the ornament has a luminous quality.

**The eyes.** The eyes are painted last. Once they are complete, the painting is seen as a true representation of the deity. A consecration often follows, in which mantra syllables are painted on the back.

**Mounting.** The painting is trimmed and stitched to a brocade mount with set proportions, with a wooden rod at the bottom that weights it so that it hangs flat and helps to roll it up.

## What to look for

- **Even, unbroken lines,** especially in faces and hands.
- **Proportions that follow the grid,** so that the figure looks settled and not stretched.
- **Colour laid evenly,** with soft, gradual shading.
- **Gold used for ornament and outline,** with some of it polished.
- **The same care at the edges** as at the centre.

## Caring for it

A thangka is kept rolled and covered when it is not on display, in a dry place where damp will not reach the silk. [Read the painted-cloth care guide](/care/painted-cloth).
